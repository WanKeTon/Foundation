## A B S T R A C T

Extreme weather events have destructive impacts on ecosystems and are expected to increase. Understanding how forest ecosystems respond to climate extremes has attracted much attention in recent decades. However, the mechanisms of climate extreme under various geological conditions are still unclear and difficult to quantify. Here, we selected four extreme weather events, namely, compound dry–hot extremes, compound wet–hot ex tremes, compound dry–cold extremes, and extreme precipitation, to explore the memory effects of vegetation after these extremes occurred. We combined satellite observations and climate data to quantify the resistance, tolerance and resilience of vegetation using lag response time, memory strength and memory length, respectively. Our results showed that among these events, compound dry–hot extremes were the most crucial climate stressors in the Red River Basin due to longer memory length and higher memory strength. There is a trade-off between resistance (lag response time), tolerance (memory strength) and resilience (memory length). Compared with grassland, arbor and shrub forests exhibit a higher resistance and tolerance to drought and a relatively lower resilience. The tolerance to extreme precipitation of grassland surpasses that of forest, while the resistance is inferior. The vegetation in the carbonate distribution area has higher tolerance and resilience and a weaker resistance than that in the non-carbonate area. Compound hot events may accentuate the severity of the influence exerted by both extreme drought and extraordinary precipitation on vegetation. These results highlight the unique extreme weather responses of karst vegetation and supplement the theory of vegetation extreme weather response strategies.

## 1. Introduction

Climate extremes and associated climate changes are sudden hydrologic and climatic conditions that exert pervasive influences on human society and terrestrial ecosystem dynamics (Chai et al., 2021). Recent studies have indicated that global temperatures will continue to increase, leading to more extreme events (e.g., heavy precipitation, droughts, and heatwaves) (Ricke and Caldeira, 2014; Yi, 2021; Wang et al., 2021). As a result, widespread extreme weather may become the new normal and profoundly impact ecosystem functions by reducing vegetation (Schwalm et al., 2017; Rais et al., 2021; Orimoloye et al., 2021; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2020). As a large and persistent carbon sink, forest ecosystems provide most of the terrestrial primary productivity, but terrestrial ecosystems are sensitive to climate change. To cope with adverse effects on forest ecosystems, vegetation change has become a valuable means for studying the interaction between climate change and forest ecosystems due to its indicative and sensitive nature.

Current evidence indicates that the resilience and resistance of vegetation, defined in terms of memory effects, simultaneously exist after climate extremes (He et al., 2018; Hossain et al., 2022; Canarini et al., 2021). Resilience has been determined as the speed at which ecosystems recover to their original state after extremes (Mitra et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2018). Resistance refers to the ability to maintain an unchanged state under drought disturbances (Hossain et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2021). Given the memory length and response lag time (i.e., memory effects), resilience and resistance can be accurately quantified based on various vegetation indices (NDVI, EVI, EVI, GPP, NPP). The spatialization outcomes derived from these indices aptly depict the spatial heterogeneity characterizing vegetation’s response to extreme weather conditions (Liu et al., 2023). Defined as the time needed to adapt to changes in the environment, memory length has been widely used to characterize the degree of resilience of vegetation to climatic or anthropogenic stressors (Arani et al., 2021). Previous studies have used various data, such as tree ring records, gross primary productivity (GPP) observations, and satellite data, to calculate memory length (Yao et al., 2022). Due to their longer observation times and convenient methods, satellite-based techniques have been identified as the most suitable method for large-scale research (Luo et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020; Deng et al., 2020; De Keersmaecker et al., 2015; Verbesselt et al., 2016). In addition to the resilience of vegetation, resistance has also attracted great attention and has been quantified by the response lag time in recent studies (Shi et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2019; Tang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2020). Certain scholarly inquiries have also analyzed vegetation changes during extremes. Within the confines of this discourse, we embark upon a quantification of this phenomenon through memory strength, a parameter herein designated as "tolerance." Above all, the reaction mechanism of forest ecosystems can be thoroughly analyzed and can provide another perspective for judging the memory effect (Liu et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2022).

Numerous studies have suggested that the changes in post-extreme forest ecosystems differ spatially (Fang et al., 2020; Zeng et al., 2022; Qi et al., 2022; Seixas et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2022). For example, the response lag time of plants after high temperatures at middle and high latitudes (Tibetan Plateau) is longer than that in low-latitude regions in global terrestrial ecosystems (Southwest China) (Li et al., 2021; He et al., 2021; Chai et al., 2021). Moreover, recovery after drought is the longest in the tropics and high northern latitudes (both of which are vulnerable areas of Earth’s climate system) compared with other regions of global terrestrial ecosystems (Schwalm et al., 2017). From a topographical standpoint, the vegetation in the Swiss forests reveals lower resistance and heightened resilience at lower elevations and on south-facing slopes after drought (Sturm et al., 2022). Conversely, in the southwestern region of China, vegetation at elevated altitudes demonstrates elevated resistance levels (Liu et al., 2022). Based on the comparison of these previous studies, there are significant distinctions in the vegetation extreme climate response in southwestern China compared to other re gions of the world. Previous studies have shown that the southwestern region of China is not only one of the regions with the most obvious greening in the world, but also the regions with the most concentrated karst distribution (Zhu et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2021). Therefore, we have reason to assume that the difference in extreme climate response between Southwest China and other regions around the world may be caused by realms of vegetation or geology.

One possible reason for this spatial heterogeneity is that there may be significant discrepancies among the memory effects of different vegetation forms in response to climate changes (Cao et al., 2022; Matkala et al., 2021; Ying et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020). More specifically, previous studies have shown that the response lag time of grasslands to climate is significantly shorter than that of forests and that grasslands can recover from the impact of climate change faster (Qi et al., 2022). It is believed that while forests can implement measures to resist climate change earlier due to their deeper root systems, once their physiology is affected, it is difficult for them to recover from such effects. Conversely, while grasslands are most likely more vulnerable to drought, they can recover more quickly (Ying et al., 2020). Hence, the water use strategies of different vegetation types may lead to a trade-off relationship between resistance and resilience and trigger postextreme spatial hetero geneity in vegetation change (Li et al., 2020; Perfecto et al., 2019; Yao et al., 2022; Carriere et al., 2020; Hao et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2022).

Additionally, geological conditions may contribute to differences in vegetation memory effects after extreme weather by affecting the hydrologic cycle and vegetation root growth. In carbonate rock regions, vegetation is vulnerable to environmental change due to the thin soil layer and high regolith water loss rate of strongly weathered bedrock. In fact, in many areas, especially subtropical karst areas, extreme weather events frequently occur over a short period (several years) (Tao et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2020). Although existing studies have revealed the characteristics of memory effects on climate change in various ecosystems worldwide, the memory effects of karst vegetation are still ambiguous (Li et al., 2021c). Furthermore, there are many ecologically fragile karst areas worldwide (Zhao et al., 2020). Due to their unique geological conditions, vegetation in karst regions exhibits a higher sensitivity to climate change, especially changes in precipitation (Carriere et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2018). In the face of climate change and climate extremes in particular, rocky desertification, which is the phenomenon of carbonate exposure caused by vegetation degradation beyond the elastic limit, has become the primary ecological problem in karst areas (Jiang et al., 2020). Therefore, research on the extreme weather memory effect of vegetation in karst areas has practical significance for ecological management and protection.

Southwest China is one of the main areas of karst distribution, and the ecosystems in this region are easily damaged and have extreme difficulty recovering from disturbances (Zhao and Hou, 2019; Zhong et al., 2022). In recent years, observations have shown that meteoro logical drought has increased since 1970 and that the increase has become more obvious since 2000 (Sillmann et al., 2013). In fact, due to climate change and the El Nino ˜ phenomenon, the dry season has become longer, and the rainy season has become shorter, resulting in the superposition of high temperatures and the dry season; this combination of dryness and heat has increased the harshness of the regional drought (Nerantzaki and Nikolaidis, 2020). Compared with joint dry–hot conditions, dry–cold extremes are more common and are mainly concentrated in November–April. Therefore, various climate extremes (including compound dry–hot extremes, compound wet–hot extremes, compound dry–cold extremes and extreme precipitation.) have become increasingly serious in southwestern China during recent decades and pose a severe threat to the vegetation in the area (Li et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2020). Several studies have reported that the vegetation increase rate in karst regions is much lower than that in non-karst regions (Zhang et al., 2022; Cai et al., 2022). In the Red River Basin, a typical Karst Plateau basin with a fragile ecology (serious rocky desertification), these extreme weather events have caused serious impacts. However, the mechanism behind this phenomenon remains unclear and requires more comprehensive discussion.

Based on this background, we used meteorological data and Mod erate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) remote sensing image data of China’s Red River Basin from 2000 to 2020 to explore the impact of four specific extreme weather events on vegetation in Southwest China. Furthermore, spatial statistical methods were used to reveal the characteristics of memory effects under various vegetation types or geological and memory effects, which provides a way to understand the coping strategies of vegetation to extreme climate in both karst and nonkarst regions. This study contributes to a clearer understanding of vegetation succession and is also beneficial for proposing suggestions for decision-makers.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Study area

The Red River Basin is a watershed located in southwestern Yunnan Province, China, and its area is approximately $8 . 2 8 \ \times \ 1 0 ^ { 4 }$ km<sup>2</sup> (100.09–106.98◦E, 22.39–25.58◦N). The elevation ranges from 71 m to approximately 3123 m. The geomorphology of the study area is divided into two units, namely, the Yunnan Plateau in the east and Hengduan Mountain in the west, with the Red River Valley in the middle (Gu et al., 2018). The Red River Basin has a plateau monsoon climate with an average annual temperature of 22.5 ◦C and an average annual

The vegetation is mainly composed of the Ailao Mountain–Red River vegetation: the west side is dominated by evergreen broad-leaved and evergreen coniferous forests, and the east is dominated by broad-leaved forests and grasslands (Fig. 1) (Li et al., 2021a). Due to low human activity, there is a large amount of primary forest in the area and on both sides of the Ailao Mountains (Li et al., 2021).

The Red River Basin is one of the most typical Karst Plateau basins in China, which is not reflected in its karst area but in the strong karstification and rocky desertification process. Because of the presence of karst landforms with relatively thin soil layers and severe soil erosion, the red soil in the karst area of the Red River Basin enters the river due to water erosion, resulting in the red or dark red color of the Red River (Kazmierczak et al., 2022). Therefore, the Red River Basin has become the most typical Karst Plateau basin with the most severe ecological problems in Southwest China, as validated by the Bulletin on the Status of Rocky Desertification in Karst Areas of China (https://www.forestry. gov.cn).

In the past 20 years, many extreme weather events have occurred in the Red River Basin. Coupled with the fragile ecological environment of the karst area, this has resulted in significant changes in the vegetation of the Red River Basin. Therefore, it is essential to quantify the potential impact of extreme weather events on the vegetation and provide decision-makers with practical recommendations for dealing with extreme weather.

## 2.2. Data sources

The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) was used to characterize vegetation coverage in this study, and the data were accessed from MODIS MOD13Q1. This vegetation index product is produced by the NASA EOS datacenter (https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis. nasa.gov). MOD13Q1 is a 16-day composite product, which means that there are 23 images each year for the same location. Its spatial resolution is approximately 250 m. The maximum NDVI values were chosen to represent the monthly NDVI time series (Zhao et al., 2020) with a spatial resolution of approximately 250 m, which provide an excellent basis to study long-term vegetation dynamics as well as vegetation responses to disturbances such as drought (Keyserlingk et al.,2021). In addition, total daily temperature ( ◦C) and precipitation (mm) data from 14 meteorological stations in the study area were collected from the National

Climate Center (NCC) of the China Meteorological Administration (CMN) (Yu et al., 2020). Most meteorological data are 3-hour data, and a small number of stations have 1-hour data, all of which are calculated as daily average data from 2000 to 2020. We used the kriging interpolation method to spatialize these data with the help of ACRGIS 10.0. Among the 14 selected sites, multiple sites around the karst concentrated distribution area were included to ensure that the interpolated data can accurately represent the climate of the karst area as much as possible. Additionally, vegetation types and lithology data from rocky desertification monitoring data in Yunnan Province were used to determine the memory effect characteristics of extreme weather under different vegetation types and lithology conditions.

## 2.3. Methods

In our study, the methods included four parts. First, the temperature and precipitation data from 14 stations in the study area were input into the RclimeDEX model, and indices from the Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI) were obtained, including Tx10p (days), Tx90p (day), Rx5day (mm), and R20 (days). Second, this study selected points that deviated far from the fitted curve based on the univariate regression fitting data of extreme climate indicators each year and excluded points where other extreme weather events occurred within twelve months (before and after) of an event to obtain the typical extreme weather occurrence time. Third, we used residual analysis and Mann–Kendall (MK) mutation analysis to establish an analytical model of extreme weather memory effects and calculate the extreme weather memory effects of vegetation, including the response lag time, memory length and memory strength. Fourth, we use the root mean square de viation (RMSD) method proposed by Bradford et al. (2012) to measure the trade-offs between resilience (memory length), tolerance (memory strength) and resistance (response lag time).

## 2.3.2. Selection of typical extreme weather event

We divided extreme weather events into compound dry–hot extremes, compound wet–hot extremes, compound dry–cold extremes and extreme precipitation. We plotted smooth curves for Tx10p, Tx90p, Rx5day, and R20 to select for extreme weather. We follow the following principles:

(i) The extreme weather index is at the peak, and no other extreme weather events have occurred in the year before and after. If the extreme temperature index and extreme precipitation index overlap, it is considered a composite extreme weather event.

(ii) The selection of composite extreme weather follows the principle of controlling variables. The high temperature indices (Tx90P) of the compound dry–hot extreme and compound wet–hot extreme were approximately equal, while the precipitation indices (Rx5day, R20) of dry–cold and precipitation extremes are approximated.

## 2.3.3. Vegetation memory effect of typical extreme weather events

In this study, the time-lag effect of monthly extreme climate and vegetation evolution was investigated. According to previous research results, the memory effect of vegetation on climate lasts one year (Tang et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2021). Thus, this paper considers only the climate-related memory effect of vegetation during and one year after the occurrence of an extreme weather event.

We used residual analysis to quantify the impact of extreme weather on vegetation. In ecology, residual analysis calculates the impact of extreme weather on vegetation changes by building a regression model. By considering that both the extent of major ecological restoration projects in the Red River Basin and farmland and urban areas during 2000–2020 were excluded from our research, we separated the Red River Basin into “basically natural areas”, where vegetation changes are mainly affected by climate change, and “human-disturbed areas”, where vegetation is driven by both climate and human disturbance (Chen et al., 2021).

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Annual correlation patterns between the NDVI and extreme climate indices

Time series analysis of the NDVI showed a statistically significant greening trend in the study area, with a mean slope increase of 0.028/ 10a from 2000 to 2020. All precipitation indices, Rx5day and R20, show a slow upward trend. The frequency index of extreme low temperatures decreased significantly (TX10p), while the frequency index of extreme high temperatures increased (TX90p). These results show that climate change in the Red River Basin is consistent with global climate change and that the local climate is moving in a wetter and hotter direction (Thackeray et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2020).

The correlation between the monthly maximum NDVI and extreme climatic indices was calculated to identify the relationship between extreme climate variables and vegetation dynamics. The results of the spatial correlation analysis showed that the positive correlation coefficient between TX90P and NDVI was more than 73 %, and the areas with significant correlations $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ accounted for 47 % of the total area $( \mathrm { F i g . 3 a ) }$ . In contrast, the NDVI was negatively correlated with the cold factor TX10P in most regions, accounting for 61 % of the total area. In terms of extreme precipitation stress, the NDVI was significantly positively correlated with precipitation factors R20 and Rx5day in most study areas, accounting for 62 % and 51 % of the total area, respectively. Overall, those ETCCDI indices can demonstrate the trend of climate change and the intensity of extreme weather events, but they cannot fully describe the impact of extreme weather events on vegetation.

## 3.2. Memory effect of vegetation dynamics after four typical extreme weather event

The memory effect of vegetation after extreme weather events was investigated and mapped (Figs. 4–6). The spatial patterns of MS over the Red River Basin showed spatial heterogeneity among different extremes. Compared to other extremes, the compound dry–hot extremes had significantly higher MS, and this phenomenon is more easily observed in high-elevation areas in the eastern part of the watershed. In contrast, the upper MS of the compound dry–cold extreme is mainly distributed in the flat areas of the western region. In addition, the spatial distributions of compound wet–hot extreme and extreme precipitation events are similar, both evenly distributed throughout the entire watershed, without showing strong spatial heterogeneity.

From the perspective of response lag time, vegetation mostly responds within four months after four typical extreme weather events, especially for two extreme precipitation events. Compared to two extreme precipitation events, vegetation after compound dry–hot and dry–cold events tend to choose longer response lag times to increase vegetation resistance. Vegetation after experiencing extreme precipitation has shorter lag response time than vegetation after experiencing extreme drought events (Fig. 5).

Different from compound dry–cold extremes and extreme precipi tation, the two hot compound events showed longer memory times, with an average memory time of more than six months, which indicates that the combination of thermal events may exacerbate the impact of extreme dryness and precipitation on vegetation, making it difficult for vegetation to quickly recover from extremes (Fig. 6).

## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Changes in vegetation under typical extreme weather conditions

Our research elucidates the disparities in the impacts of four extreme weather events on vegetation. Analogous to antecedent inquiries, the compound dry–hot extreme episode of 2009/2010 emerged as the most severe climatic perturbation over the past two decades within the Red River Basin, distinguished by its profound influence and protracted duration (Song et al., 2019; Wang et al.,2019). Compared with dry–cold composite events, this dry–hot event exhibited a more pronounced impact in terms of memory length and memory intensity. Similarly, although there was no significant difference in precipitation intensity (the value of Rx5day was within 55±2 mm) between the two excessive precipitation events, the memory length of wet–hot extremes were much longer than that of the 2017/2018 non composite extreme precipitation event. We are inclined to postulate that compound hot events may accentuate the severity of the influence exerted by both extreme drought and extraordinary precipitation on vegetative ecosystems, although distinct vegetation types inherently harbor diverse thermal predilections arising from their structural and functional variances. Within elevated thermal regimes, the heightened respiration of vegetation engenders growth suppression and even mortality, particularly under hypoxic circumstances accentuated by copious precipitation. Conversely, under drought conditions, elevated temperatures induce intensified evaporation, thereby compounding the deleterious effects of water scarcity. Therefore, the aggravating effect of compound hot events on vegetation needs to be further studied in the future.

## 4.2. Trade-off relationship among resilience, resistance and tolerance of vegetation after extremes

Several studies describe how disturbances (climate extremes and human destruction) drive ecosystem responses and the trade-off relationship between resilience and resistance (Liu et al., 2020; Hossain and Li, 2021). When imposed management practices lead to increased ecosystem resistance, resilience will be weakened (Yu et al., 2022). More specifically, low-resilience forests can resist drought for a period, while grassland exhibit the opposite trend (high resilience, low resistance) under the same conditions. Ecosystems, organisms, and processes all exhibit a resistance–resilience trade-off (Perfecto et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2021). As shown in our study, the vegetation after extremes exhibited a trade-off relationship (both between lag time and memory length and between lag time and memory strength) due to the discrepancy in vegetation strategies to extreme weather. In departure from prior theories, our study posits that the intricate choreography of vegetation’s responses to climatic extremes comprises an equilibrium among the attributes of resistance, tolerance, and resilience, which are represented and quantified by lag response time, memory strength and resilience. We found that forest ecosystems exhibit a predilection for heightened resistance after drought events and tend to enhance their capacity for resilience in the wake of extreme precipitation. Indeed, within the majority of our research area, vegetation deliberately opts to relinquish its leaf vitality, thus ensuring both resistance and resilience in the subsequent process. We noted that the gain proportions of memory strength, lag response time and memory length in the trade-off among them were 1.11 %, 83.33 % and 15.56 % (Fig 9) for drought extremes. For the two precipitation events, the order of the trade-off gain ratio from high to low is memory length (61.11 %), lag response time (37.78 %) and memory strength (1.11 %). This result further confirmed that ecosystems have a trade-off relationship among resistance, tolerance, and resilience.

Our research has also validated this conclusion from two aspects: different vegetation types and different lithology. We found that forestland exhibits a higher resistance and tolerance to drought than grassland, meaning that forests can respond to drought for a long period and have less impact on their physiological state. In contrast, grasslands have higher resilience and tolerance to heavy rainfall, but their resis tance is relatively low.

The difference in extreme response strategies between forests and grassland can be interpreted through the physiological characteristics of the vegetation. Due to the strong absorption capacity of water in deep soil, forests have sufficient water and strong drought resistance in the short term, but they are not well adapted to excessive precipitation (Wei et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Conversely, with root systems near the soil surface, grassland can recover soon from excessive precipitation but struggles in response to drought because of the high evaporation rate (Hu et al., 2022). Obviously, a single type of vegetation distribution may weaken an area’s resilience and resistance to disasters because of the complementary relationships among the extreme weather response times of different vegetation types (Hulvey et al., 2013; Gu et al., 2018).

We attributed the higher viability of mixed-tree areas to numerous mechanisms, including reduced intraspecific competition and resource-use complementarity (Ngaba et al., 2019). Almost all current afforestation projects fail to restore biodiversity to levels approximating native levels (Hua et al., 2016). However, mixed tree stands can increase biodiversity and provide resilience and resistance after extremes, and they cost less than pure forests to cultivate (Hua et al., 2016). Therefore, we believe that a more complex vegetation configuration may be an important means for improving the disaster viability of vegetation after ecological restoration.

Geological conditions also have a significant impact on the change in vegetation by controlling the response of vegetation after extremes from the bottom up due to the differences in water and soil resource use strategies (Tong, 2017, 2018). Our research suggested that vegetation in karst areas exhibits a faster response than vegetation in non-karst areas, irrespective of whether it is confronted by extraordinary precipitation or drought events, while its memory strength and memory length are lower. The vegetation extreme response strategies in karst areas, characterized by low resistance and high resilience and tolerance, can be explained by the geological characteristics. The “ever-wet” non-karst areas need some time for water evaporation (infiltration) after extremes and a period of absorption (evaporation) to recover correspondingly (Yang et al., al.,2022), while the characteristics of rapid evaporation and infiltration in karst areas with thin soil layers make them very sensitive to extreme climates. On the other hand, under the dual effects of a deep vegetation root system and rapid water loss, negative effects on vegetation caused by water limitation or saturation in karst areas due to the buffering effect of soil are weakened or even disappear (Li et al., 2021). Therefore, we believe that extreme weather is not the reason for the slower rate of greening in karst areas. Compared to that in non-karst areas, vegetation in karst areas is less affected by extreme weather in terms of memory length, memory intensity, and lag response time.

## 5. Conclusions

In this study, we amalgamated the dimensions of resistance (lag response time), tolerance (memory strength) and resilience (memory length) to formulate a memory effect system that describes the vegeta tion response strategies to extreme weather conditions. Given this systematic framework, we analyzed and compared vegetation memory effects of four typical extreme weather events witnessed in the Red River Basin in southwestern China. Among these events, compound dry–hot extremes were the most crucial climate stressors in the Red River Basin due to longer memory length and higher memory strength. Compared with grassland, arbor and shrub forests exhibit a higher resistance and tolerance to drought, accompanied by relatively lower resilience. The tolerance to extreme precipitation of arbor and shrub forests surpasses that of grassland, while their resilience is lower. The vegetation in the carbonate distribution area has higher tolerance and resilience than that in the non-carbonate area, accompanied by a relatively weak resistance. Compound hot events may accentuate the severity of the influence exerted by both extreme drought and extraordinary precipitation on vegetation. There is a trade-off among resistance (lag response time), tolerance (memory strength) and resilience (memory length). Ou memory effect quantification method provides methodological guidance for assessing the extreme weather responses of vegetation and supplements the theoretical foundation of vegetation response strategies to extreme weather.

In addition, by comparing the memory effects of vegetation after four extreme weather events, we found that compound hot events may increase the destructive effects of drought and extreme precipitation on vegetation. However, this inference still requires a large amount of data analysis in the future and represents our new research direction.
